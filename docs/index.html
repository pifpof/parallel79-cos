AI that actually remembers.

Most AI tools start from zero every conversation. Your Chief of Staff is different.

Your conversation is working memory.
Your vault is long-term memory.
"Save to memory" is the save button.

When you talk to your Chief of Staff, everything is live but temporary. When you save to memory, what mattered gets written down permanently. Next time, it's still there. That single habit, start the session and save at the end, is what separates a system that compounds from a very capable chatbot.

Your vault
Structured Markdown files on your computer. Grows with every session. Queryable, auditable, yours.

Up and running in five minutes.

Follow these steps in order. You only do step one once.

  1. 1

    Install the skill pack

    In any Claude Code or Cowork session, run /plugin marketplace add pifpof/parallel79-cos, then /plugin install ai-chief-of-staff@parallel79. On any other platform, run npx github:pifpof/parallel79-cos.

  2. 2

    Create your vault folder

    Create an empty folder anywhere on your computer, for example ~/my-chief-of-staff/. Open Claude Code (or your agentic tool) inside it. This folder becomes your knowledge base.

  3. 3

    Run /onboard

    Type /onboard. This is a first meeting with your Chief of Staff, not a setup wizard. You'll define how they should work with you, then brief them on your role, priorities and world. Take your time: the depth of this conversation shapes everything that follows.

  4. 4

    Start each session with /start

    Every time you open a new session, type /start. Your Chief of Staff reads a short card of what matters right now and the map of its memory, then opens with a brief on where things stand. It stays quick however much it remembers, and pulls in more as the conversation goes. You never have to catch it up. Need something extra for this session, such as a slow connector check or a calendar pull? Add it to the command: /start also check our project tracker.

  5. 5

    End each session with /save

    Before you close, type /save. Your Chief of Staff writes what changed back to the vault: decisions made, priorities shifted, anything new. This is the habit the system depends on. Without it, each session starts from scratch.

The one thing that matters most: the save habit. The biggest difference between people who get compounding value and people who don't is whether they type /save before closing. A system you don't save to is just a very capable chatbot.

Ten commands. Complete coverage.

You don't need to memorise these. You'll learn them by using them. Here's the full set.

Getting started

Start here, once
/onboard

Onboard

A two-phase hiring conversation. First, you define the persona, style and name of your Chief of Staff. Then you brief them on who you are: your role, organisation, priorities, recurring workflows and key people. At the end, the vault holds your profile, the memory map and your first card of what matters now, and the system is ready to work.

Run this once before any other skill. The depth and specificity of your answers here set the quality of every session that follows. Don't rush it.

Tip: if you're vague, the system will be vague. Be specific about your priorities: not "grow revenue" but "close the Series A by Q3 with a lead investor already identified".

Working with your assistant

Daily · session start
/start

Start session

Reads a short card of what matters right now, plus the map of everything it knows, and opens with a sharp brief: where things stand, what's on the radar and a suggested focus for today. You never have to catch it up. It's also where you go beyond the standing rules in CLAUDE.md: add extra instructions to the command (/start also check our project tracker) when a session needs a slower lookup that shouldn't run every time.

When: at the top of every working session. Pair it with /save at the end. Formerly /load.

Daily · session end
/save

Save to memory

Synthesises everything significant from the session (shifted priorities, decisions made, new context, new people) and writes it back to the vault. Updates the folder indexes, then rewrites the short card of what matters right now, which is what your next /start reads. This is the keystone skill: without it, nothing accumulates.

The rule: never close a session without typing /save. The system is only as good as what got written back last time.

Mid-session reset
/recenter

Recenter

Why it exists: in a long session, your Chief of Staff can slowly lose its edge. It gets softer, hedges more and starts sounding like a generic assistant. The main cause is the conversation being condensed automatically to save space: the summary keeps the facts but can drop the instructions about who your Chief of Staff is and how it should work with you. Long stretches of data pulls dilute it the same way.

What it does: re-reads its operating instructions and your working style, checks its recent replies for directness, honest pushback and tone, and if it had drifted, tells you in one line and corrects course. Real drift is logged in your working-style file, so recurring patterns become visible.

When: it runs on its own after a long conversation is condensed. Type /recenter yourself whenever it doesn't sound like itself.

Ingest
/capture

Capture a source

Hand it something to remember: a URL, a report, a document or pasted text. It reads the source, pulls out what matters to you specifically, files the raw material in /data and compiles the knowledge into the wiki, cross-linked to what's already in memory. This is how the library grows.

Examples: a competitor's product update, an industry report, an article you want to remember. For meetings, use /minutes.

Ingest · meetings
/minutes

Meeting minutes

Turns a meeting into minutes, action items and memory. It pulls the transcript from a connected meeting tool like Granola, or takes a pasted transcript or a quick spoken debrief (/minutes debrief). It strips the small talk and anything sensitive, writes clean minutes to /outputs/meetings and sorts action items from your seat: what you owe, what you're waiting on and what others owe each other. It updates people, projects and decisions in memory and, once you approve, sends your to-dos to your task system. Raw transcripts never land in the vault.

When: after any meeting that mattered, or /minutes today at the end of the day. Settings live in memory/reference/meetings.md. Until you point it at a task system, to-dos go to outputs/follow-ups.md.

Retrieval
/query

Query the wiki

Ask a question and get an answer drawn from your own accumulated knowledge, not the open internet. "What did we decide about the vendor contract?" "Pull together everything we know about this client." Answers are grounded and traceable back to their sources. The longer the system runs, the better these answers get.

The difference: an answer from your compiled wiki, informed by months of context, beats anything you'd type into a search engine.

Keeping memory up to date

Ingest · batch
/sync

Sync sources

The batch version of /capture. Dropped a stack of files into /data without processing them? Run /sync and the system catches up: it finds unprocessed and changed sources, reads them oldest first and compiles everything into the wiki.

When: after a stretch of offline work, or when several documents have piled up that you want absorbed at once.

Maintenance
/health-check

Health check

A tune-up. Scans memory for files missing metadata, stale projects still marked active, sources that were never compiled, broken lineage, contradictions, index drift and a stale or oversized startup card. It rebuilds the indexes and the card from your files, tells you how much /start reads, fixes what it safely can and surfaces the judgement calls that only you can make.

When: weekly or monthly. A clean vault means sharper briefs, better answers and faster starts.

Stepping back

Weekly
/review

Weekly review

A step back. What moved this week, what stalled, what patterns are showing up across projects and weeks, and the single priority for the week ahead with three concrete intentions. Honest by design: a Chief of Staff who only tells you what went well isn't worth having. It saves itself to memory, so no separate /save is needed.

When: end of the week. The patterns section is where the system earns its keep. It sees things across weeks that you're too close to notice.

Just a folder of files.

Your knowledge base is plain Markdown on your own computer. No lock-in. Human-readable. It outlasts the tooling.

/data

Raw sources

Every document, article or note the system has ingested. Append-only: nothing is edited after it lands. This is the source of truth and the lineage anchor for everything in memory.

/memory

The wiki

Structured, interlinked Markdown that compiles the raw sources into knowledge, organised into /profile, /projects and /library. This is the layer that compounds.

/memory/profile

Your profile

Who you are, your current priorities, recurring workflows, key people and working style. Seeded by /onboard and updated by /save as things change.

/memory/projects

Projects

Active and reference project knowledge. Decisions logged, blockers noted, status tracked. This is where your Chief of Staff holds the thread on everything you're working on.

/memory/library

Library

Retained knowledge, research, reference material and a decisions log. Written by /capture and /sync as sources are compiled into the wiki.

/outputs

Outputs

Generated content worth keeping: briefs, query answers, weekly reviews and meeting minutes in /outputs/meetings. Named with dates, so your history is auditable.

memory.md

Memory map

One row per memory folder: what lives there, when to open it, and where its own index is. Each folder keeps an _index.md with one line per file. Every skill starts from the map and finds files by searching, so the system stays fast as the vault grows.

memory/_state.md

Today's card

A one-screen snapshot: this week's #1 goal, active projects and their next step, open decisions, overdue items, last review. Written by /save and /review, read first by /start. If it falls behind, /start notices and /health-check rebuilds it.

/trash

Trash

Things removed from context but never hard-deleted. Keeping a file costs almost nothing; losing something important costs a lot.

Tip: see your vault in Obsidian. Open the vault folder in Obsidian (opens in a new tab) and your memory becomes a graph you can browse and click through. You don't need it to use the system, but it's a satisfying way to watch your knowledge base grow.

Four habits that make the difference.

The system rewards consistent use. These habits separate a compounding knowledge base from an expensive chat window.

01

Save, every time.

The biggest difference between people who get value from this system and people who don't is the save habit. If something significant happened in a conversation, such as a decision, a new priority or new context, save it before you leave. A system you don't save to is just a chatbot.

02

Feed it what matters.

The more you capture, the smarter the system gets. When you read something that matters to your business, like a competitor's announcement or a market report, hand it over with /capture. After a meeting that mattered, run /minutes. Every source makes every future answer sharper.

03

Talk to it like a colleague.

It has context. Use it. "What should I be worried about going into the board meeting?" is a better question than anything you'd type into Google, because it knows your board, your business and your last three months.

04

Let it push back.

A good Chief of Staff doesn't just take orders. It notices patterns, flags what's being avoided and tells you what you might not want to hear. The weekly review is designed to be honest with you. Don't soften it by only asking easy questions.

The architecture that makes it compound.

No vector database, no embeddings, no vendor lock-in. Just Markdown files and an index.

/dataAppend-only · source of truth

Raw sources. Every document or article that enters the system lands here first and is never edited afterwards. This is the audit trail: any memory file can be traced back to a raw source in this folder.

/memoryLLM-maintained · compounds over time

The compiled wiki. Structured, interlinked Markdown that distils the raw sources into knowledge. The model reads it, writes to it and updates it, but it's also human-readable. If the AI tooling disappears tomorrow, the knowledge survives. This is the Karpathy LLM wiki pattern: compile knowledge ahead of time, not at query time.

CLAUDE.mdSchema · persona · rules

The schema. Defines who your Chief of Staff is, how the vault is structured, the rules it works by and the full skill list. This is the most important file in the system: most customisation happens here. Every session starts by reading it.

memory.mdMap · read first

The map. One line per memory folder: what lives there, when to open it, and where its own index is. Next to it, a short card (memory/_state.md) holds what matters right now. A session starts from the map and the card, then finds anything else by searching. That's what keeps startup flat as the vault grows: the system gets smarter without getting slower.

Every memory file starts with this block.

Breaking this contract degrades every skill that reads memory. Never create a file in /memory without it.

memory/profile/priorities.md
---
title:    "Current Priorities"
summary:  "The two or three things that matter most right now."
sources:  ["onboard", "session — 2026-06-26"]
aliases:  ["priorities", "focus", "goals"]
tags:     ["active"]
updated:  "2026-06-26"
---

## Q3 priority: close the Series A
...

Keeping the vault healthy.

A vault left without maintenance slowly degrades. Most problems have simple fixes, and almost all of them come down to one of three causes.

The three most common problems, in order: the memory indexes or today's card are stale; a source wasn't compiled (an orphaned file in /data); or the executive stopped saving. Check these first.
SymptomLikely causeFix
Vault feels slow or unfocused The memory map, the card or a profile file has grown Run /health-check. It reports the startup load, rebuilds the indexes and the card, and offers to fold long files.
Queries return thin or generic answers Sources in /data were never compiled Run /health-check to find orphaned sources, then /sync to process them.
Memory contradicts itself Two files have conflicting facts /health-check surfaces it. You pick the correct version and move the wrong one to /trash with a note.
Session doesn't pick up where I left off /save wasn't run at the end of the session Nothing to recover: the conversation was working memory only. Run /save from now on.
Meeting commitments are slipping Meetings weren't run through /minutes Run /minutes since last. /start flags undebriefed meetings when a meeting tool is connected.
It sounds generic, soft or overly cautious A long session was condensed and the persona instructions were diluted Run /recenter. If it keeps happening, check the corrections at the bottom of memory/profile/style.md for the pattern.
Profile feels out of date Your role or priorities changed and the files didn't Tell your Chief of Staff what changed in any session, then run /save.
Need to recover something removed The file was moved to trash Check /trash. Nothing is ever hard-deleted, and each file there has a note on why it was removed.

Build skills for your own role.

The ten baseline skills cover the core loop. Role-specific skills are where each system diverges, and where most of the value builds up.

  1. 1

    Create a skill folder

    Inside .claude/skills/ (project scope) or ~/.claude/skills/ (global), create a folder named after your skill. Inside it, create SKILL.md. Longer reference material can go in a references/ folder next to it.

  2. 2

    Write the frontmatter first

    The description field is what triggers the skill: Claude reads it to decide whether to use it. Be generous. List the slash command and the natural phrases someone would actually say.

    Weak: "Generates a board report."
    Strong: "Use when the user says '/board-report', asks to prepare a board update, wants to generate a board deck, or needs to summarise progress for investors."

  3. 3

    Write the body in the imperative

    "Read the vault." Not "The skill should read the vault." Explain why each step matters, not just what to do: skills that understand the why make better judgement calls. Always start from memory.md and find files by searching frontmatter. When you write something new, add a line to its folder's _index.md; touch memory.md only for a new folder.

  4. 4

    Test against real prompts, then drop it in

    Try two or three realistic phrases you'd actually say. If it doesn't trigger, the problem is almost always the description: add more trigger phrases. Once it triggers correctly, the skill is live.

Worth nothing on day one. Indispensable by month three.

Give the system a month of consistent use (starting sessions, saving out, capturing what matters) and it becomes something you'd struggle to work without. Every session adds to it. Every decision you log becomes history you can draw on. Every source you capture makes the next answer sharper.

You're not setting up a tool. You're training a second brain. Show up, save your work and let it grow.
